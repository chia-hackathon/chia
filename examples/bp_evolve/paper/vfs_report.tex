\begin{abstract}
The Championship Branch Prediction Next Generation (CBP-NG) contest replaces
raw misprediction rate with \vfs{}, a voltage-frequency-scaled speedup that
folds prediction throughput, misprediction cost and predictor energy into a
single number. We treat \vfs{} not as a ranking device but as an
\emph{optimization target}, and ask what an automated search actually finds
when \vfs{} is the only signal it receives. We built a three-tier evaluation
cascade on CHIA---CBP-NG for scoring, ChampSim for port fidelity, gem5 for a
pipeline-depth sweep---and ran thirty generations of MAP-Elites over the
template parameters of the shipped TAGE predictor, scoring 86 designs on all
126 inner traces of the official set. Three results follow. First, over the
scored population \vfs{} correlates strongly with prediction throughput
($r=+0.934$) and with first-level predictor latency ($r=-0.883$), and
essentially not at all with accuracy ($r=-0.130$ against \mpki{} spanning
$5.08$--$10.08$). Second, the gem5 tier does not discriminate: core IPC at the
shipped depth varies by $0.60\%$ across 29 promoted designs, and its apparent
agreement with Tier-1 \mpki{} ($r=-0.761$) is carried by two points and
inverts to $+0.167$ when they are removed. Third, whether the depth-nine
ranking is depth-robust is decided entirely by whether the archive happens to
hold an accuracy outlier. At generation ten it held one, and every discordant
pair beyond depth twelve involved that single design; by generation twenty-nine
the search had evicted it, the archived \mpki{} spread had narrowed to
$5.23$--$5.62$, and rank agreement is exact at every depth from six to thirty.
Accuracy governs depth robustness, and accuracy is exactly what the depth-nine
score does not measure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A scoring function used to rank a fixed slate of contest entries and a scoring
function used to \emph{steer} an automated search are not the same artifact.
The first needs only to order designs a human already thought worth
submitting. The second is climbed: whatever it rewards, a search will supply,
including rewards its designers did not intend to offer. This paper audits the
CBP-NG scoring function in the second role.

\subsection{CBP-NG}
The Championship Branch Prediction Next Generation contest distributes a
simulator, a set of 168 instruction traces, and a reference predictor written
in \emph{HARCOM}, a restricted C++ subset in which storage is declared as typed
RAM arrays with explicit read and write ports and every value carries a
timestamp as well as a bit pattern, so that a predictor's latency and energy
are derived from its source rather than asserted by its author.
Entries are ranked by \vfs{}---Voltage-Frequency-Scaled speedup---which
combines three measured quantities: how many predictions the design delivers
per cycle, how many cycles it loses to mispredictions at an assumed pipeline
depth, and how much dynamic energy it spends per instruction. \vfs{} is a
deliberate improvement on ranking by misprediction rate alone, which rewards
predictors that cannot be built.

Two properties of the harness shape everything that follows, and both are
easy to miss from the contest rules alone. The predictor is called once per
\emph{instruction} rather than once per branch, and it may declare that a run
of consecutive instructions shares a single lookup, so the quantity the score
calls throughput is a front-end bandwidth rather than a core IPC. And because
HARCOM derives delay and energy from the circuit a design implies, the two
prediction latencies it reports are charged twice: once against that
bandwidth, and again against every misprediction. Section~\ref{sec:harness}
develops both. Our question is what the resulting metric rewards.

\subsection{ChampSim}
ChampSim is a trace-driven out-of-order core simulator widely used in branch
and cache prediction research. It models a real reorder buffer, so a
mispredicted branch costs what the machine's actual occupancy makes it cost. We
use ChampSim as a \emph{port-fidelity} tier: a design that scores well in
CBP-NG must be re-expressed against ChampSim's branch-predictor interface and
must reproduce its CBP-NG conditional \mpki{} within tolerance. This catches
the failure mode in which a CBP-NG design scores well because of something the
CBP-NG harness permits and no core would. The port is not free of structural
differences from the original---ChampSim offers no prediction-block hook, and
exposes one branch at a time---so the tolerance has to be the measured width
of those differences rather than a round number. Section~\ref{sec:ports}
reports how we measured it.

\subsection{gem5}
gem5's O3 model is an execution-driven, cycle-level core in which pipeline
depth is a parameter rather than a constant. CBP-NG charges misprediction at a
\emph{fixed} nine-stage penalty. Whether a ranking obtained under that
assumption survives a machine that is deeper or shallower is an empirical
question, and gem5 is where we ask it, sweeping depth over
$\{3,6,9,12,16,20,25,30\}$.

\subsection{CHIA}
CHIA is an open-source framework for agentic hardware/software co-design that
expresses a workflow as a graph of typed nodes over a Ray-based runtime, with
each node type pinned to its own container image and its own pool of cluster
resources. It is what makes a three-simulator cascade practical: CBP-NG
scoring, ChampSim porting and gem5 depth sweeps are three incompatible
toolchains, and CHIA runs them as one flow with independently scaled pools
(\texttt{cbp\_ng}, \texttt{champsim}, \texttt{gem5}), a single lineage
database, and the same graph running on a workstation or across cloud VMs.
Fig.~\ref{fig:loop} is the graph we actually run.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Branch predictor championships.} The CBP series has driven predictor
research for two decades, and the TAGE family~\cite{seznec2006tage,seznec2011tage} remains the reference against which entries are measured. The
metric has evolved from misprediction rate toward cost-aware scoring precisely
because rate alone is unphysical; CBP-NG's \vfs{} is the current endpoint of
that evolution.

\textbf{Simulator fidelity.} A long line of work establishes that
simulator-relative conclusions do not transfer freely: ChampSim, gem5 and
custom harnesses disagree in magnitude and sometimes in
order~\cite{gem5,champsim,akram2019survey}. Cross-simulator validation is
recommended practice, but is usually performed once, on a final design, rather
than being wired into the search that produced it.

\textbf{Automated architecture search.} Design-space exploration by
evolutionary and learned methods is well established, and MAP-Elites and
quality-diversity search~\cite{mouret2015illuminating,cully2015robots} are
attractive here because they retain a front of behaviorally distinct designs
rather than collapsing to a single optimum. Recent work applies large language
models to hardware design generation and repair; our harness supports an LLM
arm, but every number in this paper comes from the \emph{offline} control arm,
which mutates template parameters with no model in the loop.

\section{What the Existing Setting Lacks}

Three gaps motivate this study.

\textbf{The metric is never audited as an objective.} \vfs{} is validated as a
ranking of submitted entries. Nobody has reported what a search does when \vfs{}
is the sole gradient---which of its three inputs actually carries the
optimizer, and which are decorative. A term that changes the score by a few
parts per thousand is not a design pressure, however principled its derivation.

\textbf{Cross-simulator checks are terminal, not structural.} Validation
usually happens once at the end. But the useful question is whether the
\emph{ranking} the cheap tier produces survives the expensive one, and that
question can only be asked if the expensive tier is inside the loop, scoring
the same lineage.

\textbf{Depth is assumed, not swept.} CBP-NG's nine-stage penalty is a fixed
constant in the scoring path. A predictor that wins at depth nine may or may
not win at depth twenty-five, and no contest result reports which.

\section{What We Achieve}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A \textbf{three-tier evaluation cascade} on CHIA in which CBP-NG scoring,
ChampSim port verification and a gem5 depth sweep run as one flow over one
lineage database, so every design carries all three tiers' verdicts.
\item A \textbf{numerical audit of \vfs{}} over a search-generated population,
identifying which of its inputs the score is actually sensitive to.
\item A \textbf{depth-robustness analysis} that does not merely report that the
depth-nine ranking degrades, but isolates \emph{which design} makes it degrade
and \emph{why}.
\item A \textbf{re-scoring identity} that lets the full depth sweep be
recomputed from counters measured once, making an eight-depth sweep free
relative to one CBP-NG pass.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The CBP-NG harness}
\label{sec:harness}
What CBP-NG measures is not what a core simulator measures, and the difference
is the subject of this paper, so it is worth stating precisely.

\textbf{The interface is per instruction, not per branch.} The harness walks
the correct-path instruction stream and calls the predictor's first- and
second-level predict methods once for \emph{every} instruction, branch or not.
A design may declare that consecutive instructions share one lookup; the
instructions covered by a single lookup form a \emph{prediction block}, and the
harness retires exactly one block per cycle. A block ends at a taken branch, at
a second-level misprediction, or when the design releases it. Prediction
throughput $T$ is therefore instructions per block-cycle. It is a front-end
bandwidth, and the direct way to raise it is to cover more instructions per
lookup---not to predict better.

\textbf{Latency and energy are derived, not declared.} Each operation on a
HARCOM value charges the propagation delay of the gate network it implies and
the switching energy of the capacitance it drives, and storage must be declared
as a RAM array with an explicit port count, so a table read costs what a table
of that shape with that many ports costs. The device parameters are those of
the ASAP5 5\,nm predictive PDK at $V_{DD}=0.75$\,V. The clock is pinned at
$300$\,ps, so the picoseconds accumulated on each level's critical path become
the cycle latencies $p_1$ and $p_2$, and the femtojoules accumulated after
warm-up, divided by correct-path instructions, become \epi{}. A design cannot
assert that it is fast or cheap; it can only be.

\textbf{The three numbers are coupled.} $p_1$ and $p_2$ enter the cycle count
behind $T$ \emph{and} the misprediction penalty $C$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cpi}: a
slow second level lengthens the penalty on every misprediction, and a first
level slower than one cycle stretches every block. Nothing else constrains a
design---CBP-NG imposes no storage budget---so $(\epi{}, p_1, p_2)$ is the
entire cost model, which is why the archive is binned on those three and not
on \vfs{}.

$T$ is a \emph{throughput}, not a core IPC: it is how many predictions the
predictor supplies per cycle. $C$ is a \emph{penalty}, not a period: it is
cycles lost to mispredictions per correct-path instruction. \epi{} is
normalized so the reference predictor scores $1000$. The product $w=TC$ is the
quantity that actually appears in the score, and it has a physical reading of
its own, developed next.

\subsection{The scoring function: inputs and speedup}
A design reaches the score as exactly three numbers---$T$, $C$ and \epi{}---so
everything \vfs{} can possibly reward is a function of those three. The
constants are $\rho=0.05$, $T_{\text{ref}}=8$, $C_{\text{ref}}=0.0315$,
$\epi{}_{\text{ref}}=1000$ and a technology parameter $\alpha=1.625$, from
which two further constants are \emph{derived} rather than chosen:
\begin{equation}
\gamma=\frac{2}{\alpha-1}=3.2, \qquad
\beta=\frac{4\alpha}{(\alpha-1)^{2}}=16.64 .
\label{eq:consts}
\end{equation}
Sections~\ref{sec:tech} and \ref{sec:norm} show where both come from; they are
not free parameters, and a reader who treats them as tuning knobs will
misread the metric.

The first derived quantity is
\begin{equation}
w \;=\; T\,C, \qquad w_{\text{ref}} = T_{\text{ref}}C_{\text{ref}} = 0.252 .
\label{eq:wpi}
\end{equation}
Despite appearances this is not an abstract product. If the machine loses $C$
cycles per correct-path instruction and fetches $T$ instructions in each of
them, it fetched $TC$ \emph{wrong-path} instructions that were later squashed.
So $w$ is the fetch amplification: the reference core fetches $1.252$
instructions for every one it retires, and roughly a fifth of its front-end
work is discarded. Naming $w$ correctly matters, because it makes the speedup
term collapse to one idea. Written as in the reference implementation,
\begin{equation}
S \;=\; \frac{T}{T_{\text{ref}}}\cdot\frac{1+w_{\text{ref}}}{1+w},
\label{eq:speedup}
\end{equation}
it looks like a throughput ratio with a correction bolted on. Regrouped, it is
a single ratio,
\begin{equation}
S \;=\; \frac{T/(1+w)}{T_{\text{ref}}/(1+w_{\text{ref}})},
\label{eq:speedup2}
\end{equation}
of \emph{useful} instructions per cycle---total fetch bandwidth divided by
fetch amplification. $S$ is therefore an honest architectural speedup at fixed
frequency. Accuracy is not absent from it, but it enters only through the
denominator $1+w$, where $w\!\ll\!1$: over our entire population $w$ ranges
over $0.242$--$0.540$, so even the worst design in the study loses under a
fifth of $S$ to the accuracy term, while $T$ scales $S$ proportionally.

\subsection{The technology model behind $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$}
\label{sec:tech}
The trailing bracket of the score,
\begin{equation}
F(P) \;=\; \alpha\left(1-\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+\beta/P}}\right),
\label{eq:fp}
\end{equation}
is usually quoted without interpretation. It is in fact one voltage--frequency
curve, written backwards. Substituting $u=\sqrt{1+\beta/P}$ and eliminating $u$
against the fixed-power-envelope constraint $P\,V^{2}F=1$---power is
capacitance-limited, so a design drawing normalized power $P$ at nominal
voltage may be clocked only as fast as its energy budget allows---gives, with
$\beta$ as in \eqref{eq:consts}, the closed form
\begin{equation}
\frac{f}{f_{0}} \;=\; \alpha-\frac{\alpha-1}{V/V_{0}} .
\label{eq:fv}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:fv} is the classical first-order delay model
$\text{delay}\propto V/(V-V_{\text{th}})$, and it fixes the meaning of every
constant in \eqref{eq:consts}:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{$\alpha$ is a frequency ceiling.} As $V\!\to\!\infty$,
$f/f_{0}\to\alpha$. No amount of voltage buys more than $1.625\times$ the
reference clock.
\item \textbf{$V_{0}$ is the calibration point.} At $V=V_{0}$,
\eqref{eq:fv} returns $f=f_{0}$ exactly.
\item \textbf{$(\alpha-1)/\alpha=0.3846$ is the implied threshold voltage.}
Below $0.385\,V_{0}$ the model gives $f\le0$.
\item \textbf{$\beta$ is not free.} It is the unique value for which
$F(1)=1$: at reference power the design gets exactly the reference clock. We
verified this and the inversion \eqref{eq:fv} numerically over
$P\in[0.25,4]$, recovering $P V^{2}F=1$ to machine precision.
\end{itemize}
Note that $F$ \emph{decreases} in $P$. This is the correct sign: $P$ is the
power the design would draw at nominal frequency, so a hungrier design must be
clocked down to fit the same envelope.

The same curve explains $\gamma$, which appears as $S^{\gamma}$ in the energy
term. Energy per operation scales as $V^{2}$, and inverting \eqref{eq:fv} to
$V(f)=(\alpha-1)/(\alpha-f)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln V^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\ln f}\right|_{f=1}
=\frac{2}{\alpha-1}=\gamma .
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
So $S^{\gamma}$ charges the non-predictor part of the core the
\emph{DVFS-equivalent} energy of running $S$ times faster---what the speedup
would have cost had it been bought with voltage instead of with prediction.
Two consequences deserve stating. First, this is why the energy term is
superlinear at all: without it, a faster front end would be nearly free.
Second, \eqref{eq:gamma} is a first-order identity at $f=1$ only, and the
approximation degrades away from the reference point: at $S=0.72$, typical of
our seed, $S^{\gamma}=0.350$ against an exact $V^{2}=0.477$, so the model
\emph{understates} the core energy of a slow design by a third. Every design
in this study sits in that regime.

\subsection{Normalization: $\rho$ and $\lambda$}
\label{sec:norm}
The normalized energy per instruction is
\begin{equation}
E \;=\; \left(\frac{\epi{}}{\epi{}_{\text{ref}}}\rho + \lambda S^{\gamma}\right)
        \!\left(1+\frac{w}{2}\right),
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with $\rho=0.05$ the predictor's share of core energy and $\lambda$ the
remaining core's share. The factor $(1+w/2)$ charges wrong-path work at half
the energy of a retired instruction, which is where $w$ re-enters. The value
\begin{equation}
\lambda \;=\; \bigl(1+w_{\text{ref}}/2\bigr)^{-1}-\rho \;=\; 0.838099
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
is pure normalization and carries no physics: it is chosen so that the two
shares, scaled by the wrong-path factor, come to exactly unity at the
reference point, $(0.05+0.838099)\times1.126=1.000000$.

\subsection{What the score is}
Writing $P=S\,E$---a speedup times an energy per instruction, hence a
power---the score is
\begin{equation}
\vfs{} \;=\; S\;\alpha\left(1-\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+\beta/(S E)}}\right)
       \;=\; S\cdot F(P).
\label{eq:vfs}
\end{equation}
In words: \emph{the architectural speedup at fixed frequency, times the clock
frequency that this design's power bill actually permits}. That is what
``voltage-frequency-scaled'' names. The construction has two calibration
properties that we verified exactly and that are essential to reading any
\vfs{} number:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The reference predictor scores $1.000000$.} Substituting
$(T,C,\epi{})=(8,0.0315,1000)$ yields $S=1$, $E=1$, $P=1$, $F=1$ and
$\vfs{}=1$ to every digit. A score below one therefore means \emph{worse than
the CBP-NG reference}, not merely low.
\item \textbf{The reference sits at the maximum of its own curve.} Holding $C$
and \epi{} at reference values and sweeping $T$, \vfs{} is maximized at
$T=8.05$---the reference throughput---and \emph{falls} beyond it, because
$S^{\gamma}$ eventually outruns the linear gain.
\end{enumerate}
Property~2 is the design intent of the whole metric: at the reference
operating point the elasticity of \vfs{} with respect to raw throughput is
only $+0.008$, so a contestant cannot win by widening the front end. The
metric is deliberately flat there.

Two readings follow. At the reference point the metric behaves as designed:
throughput is worth almost nothing ($+0.008$), and accuracy, at $-0.035$, is
the largest of the three. But \emph{our population never reached that point}.
Every design we scored has $T\in[3.63,6.84]$, far down the uphill flank of
Fig.~\ref{fig:tcurve}, where the throughput elasticity is $+0.28$ to $+0.60$
and exceeds the accuracy elasticity by a factor of two to four. Concretely,
from the seed a $10\%$ reduction in misprediction cost is worth $+1.49\%$
\vfs{}, while a $10\%$ increase in throughput is worth $+3.63\%$ and a $10\%$
reduction in predictor energy is worth $+0.24\%$. Even an oracle predictor
($C\!\to\!0$) buys only $+14.6\%$, less than twice what simply reaching the
reference throughput would buy ($+9.5\%$).

This is the mechanism behind every result in Section~\ref{sec:res}. \vfs{} is
not intrinsically a throughput metric; it is one \emph{in the regime where
every realistic HARCOM predictor operates}, because the flat region that makes
it balanced sits at a front-end width that none of the designs reach. An
optimizer placed in that regime will climb width, and ours did.

\subsection{Aggregation and the re-scoring identity}
Per trace, with $p_1,p_2$ the first- and second-level predictor latencies in
cycles, cycles are accumulated as
\begin{equation}
\text{cyc} =
\begin{cases}
N_p\max(1,p_2), & p_2\le p_1\\[2pt]
N_p\max(1,p_1)+N_d p_2-N_e\max(1,p_1), & p_2>p_1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
for $N_p$ predictions, $N_d$ divergences and $N_e$ divergences at end of trace,
and $T$ is the harmonic mean of $N_{\text{inst}}/\text{cyc}$ over traces. The
misprediction cost is
\begin{equation}
C \;=\; \text{mpi}\,\bigl(D + p_2 - \max(1,\min(p_1,p_2))\bigr),
\label{eq:cpi}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is pipeline depth and $\text{mpi}$ is mispredictions per instruction.
Equation~\eqref{eq:cpi} is the \emph{only} place $D$ appears. Consequently the
entire depth sweep is a closed-form re-scoring of counters measured once: eight
depths cost one CBP-NG pass, not eight. Our reimplementation reproduces
every stored \vfs{} in the study exactly---maximum absolute deviation $0$ over
all scored designs---and reproduces the stored depth sweep exactly.

\subsection{Search}
We use MAP-Elites over a behavior grid keyed on (\epi{} band, $p_1$ latency,
$p_2$ latency), nominally $6\times4\times6=144$ cells. Parents are drawn
\emph{uniformly over occupied cells}, not proportionally to \vfs{}; this is
what keeps behaviorally distinct designs alive and matters in
Section~\ref{sec:res}. The offline arm perturbs exactly two of eight template
parameters of the seed TAGE per proposal, clipped to declared ranges. It
changes no algorithm: the source diff between the seed and any offline
descendant is the struct name.

\subsection{Tiers, and what a port is allowed to move}
\label{sec:ports}
Tier~0 scores every proposal on all 126 inner traces. Tier~1 re-expresses the
Pareto front against ChampSim, at most six designs per generation. Tier~2
promotes at most two to gem5 and sweeps depth. The two ports share one file:
the algorithm is rendered once from a common template and included both by the
ChampSim branch module and by the gem5 \texttt{BPredUnit} subclass, so a
Tier-1 and a Tier-2 result about a design are results about the same code.

The ChampSim adapter itself is nearly nothing---predict on the way in, update
on the way out---but two differences from the HARCOM original are structural,
and a reader is entitled to ask whether a cross-tier comparison is comparing
predictors or comparing translations.

\textbf{The first prediction level is not ported.} In the seed design the
second level overrides the first on every instruction, so the first level
changes no prediction ChampSim could observe. It contributes to Tier~0's
latency and energy accounting and to nothing else, and porting it would add a
cost the ChampSim model cannot charge in exchange for no change in outcome.

\textbf{Global history advances at a different granularity.} CBP-NG advances
it once per prediction block; ChampSim exposes one branch at a time and offers
no block hook, so the port advances it once per branch. Two correct ports of
one predictor, fed histories that advance at different rates, compute
different indices, and until recently the only way to see the size of that
effect was to build ChampSim and simulate---which measures the port and the
core model together. We now measure it alone: a simulator-free harness renders
the shared algorithm through the same template path, compiles it, and replays
CBP-NG traces through it over Tier~0's own window. The gap is small and
one-sided, the port coming out \emph{better} by $2.1\%$ to $5.5\%$ over
designs spanning $6.2$ to $14.4$ Tier-0 \mpki{} (ratios $0.979$, $0.962$,
$0.946$, $0.945$).

The promotion gate follows that measurement rather than a round number. The
rendered algorithm must reproduce its Tier-0 \mpki{} to within $10\%$: wider
than the $5\%$ the built-simulator comparison uses, because $5\%$ would reject
correct ports at the bottom of the measured range, and still far tighter than
a translation error, which moves \mpki{} by much more than $10\%$. The
comparison is against ChampSim's \emph{conditional} \mpki{}, never the total it
prints first---the total also charges the BTB and the return-address stack,
which no Tier-0 number contains, and comparing totals would make every correct
port look some $50\%$ wrong. Tiers~0 and 1 are also driven from the same
program: the ChampSim trace is converted from the CBP-NG one, and the
conversion is checked against the instruction and branch totals CBP-NG reports
for itself, so a residual \mpki{} gap is about the predictor and not about
which workload ran.

\section{Experimental Setup}

We ran thirty generations (0--29), three variants per generation, on the
official 168-trace CBP-NG set split 126 inner / 42 held out, with every trace
run to its end and $10^{6}$ warm-up instructions. Eighty-eight designs were
proposed; 86 scored and two failed at the run stage (\texttt{gen006\_0} and
\texttt{gen007\_0}, both at the run rather than the build step). Thirty designs
reached Tier~1 and 29 reached Tier~2.

The run spans five execution segments, and the boundaries matter only for
reproducibility, not for the search: generations 0--2 ran on four
\texttt{t2d-standard-8} cloud VMs plus a local pool; generations 3--29 ran on a
single workstation with 16 concurrent CBP-NG slots. Within the local phase the
driver was restarted three times---twice because a Windows Update restarted the
host underneath the WSL2 VM---each time resuming from the newest archive
snapshot. Because MAP-Elites' search state is the archive, and each archived
elite stores its own template-parameter vector, the parameter walk continues
across every such boundary; we verified that every generation-3 variant is two
parameters from its parent, not from the seed.

Two consequences of the restarts are worth recording because they affect any
re-analysis. First, the driver derives its resume point as one past the highest
generation represented in the archive, which under-counts whenever the last
completed generation archived nothing: generation 14 archived nothing on its
first execution and was therefore re-run. Both executions are in the database;
we use the second and discard the first. Second, and consequently, variant
identifiers are unique only within an execution segment, not across the run.
Every join in this analysis is on (segment, identifier); joining on identifier
alone silently merges the two generation-14 populations.

\subsection{Why a quarter of the traces is held out}
\label{sec:heldout}
CBP-NG does not define a held-out set; the split is ours, and it exists because
the search is a search. MAP-Elites climbs \vfs{} \emph{as measured on the
traces it is scored on}, and it will supply whatever those particular traces
reward---including properties of the subset rather than of prediction. Nothing
inside the loop can tell the two apart: the archive stores the score, and the
score was computed on the inner set. An overfit to 126 programs and a genuine
improvement are the same number to every mechanism the loop has. The only
instrument that separates them is a set of traces the search was never scored
on.

The partition is a seeded shuffle of the official 168 with a fixed seed, taking
the first quarter as held out: 42 traces and $1.108\times10^{9}$ instructions
held out, 126 traces and $3.881\times10^{9}$ instructions inner. Seeding it
matters more than it looks. It makes the split a property of the trace
\emph{set} rather than of when the sweep happened, so two sweeps over the same
traces are comparable, and---because this sweep was resumed twice across a
change of cluster---a resumed run cannot silently promote a held-out trace into
the inner loop and quietly destroy the only evidence that would have caught an
overfit.

The test the split supports is a \emph{rank} test, not a score test. We rebuild
each archived elite, re-score it on the 42 held-out traces, and compute
Kendall's $\tau$ between the inner-loop ranking and the held-out ranking. The
ordering is the right object because the ordering is what the search consumed:
parents were selected, and designs promoted, on relative standing, not on
absolute \vfs{}. A front that reorders itself on unseen traces was never a
front. We report $\tau<0.6$ as an overfit to the inner subset rather than a
result. Because the test costs a full Tier-0 pass over the whole archive, it is
run once against the final archive rather than per generation.

\textbf{All \vfs{} values reported here are in-sample.} The 42 held-out traces
have not been scored, and no claim in this paper is a generalization claim.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:res}

\subsection{Finding 1: \vfs{} tracks throughput, not accuracy}

Over the 86 scored designs (Table~\ref{tab:corr}), \vfs{} correlates strongly
with prediction throughput and with first-level latency, weakly with energy,
and essentially not at all with accuracy---despite \mpki{} spanning a factor of
two, from $5.08$ to $10.08$. At $r=-0.130$ over 86 designs the accuracy
coefficient is nominally of the expected sign, but it accounts for $1.7\%$ of
the variance in \vfs{} against $87\%$ for throughput: the ordering is set by
$T$ and perturbed by nothing else that matters. This is not a small sample
effect; it is Table~\ref{tab:elast} made visible.

The best design found, \texttt{gen016\_2} at $\vfs{}=0.9288$, is the seed with
the fetch-line width \texttt{LOGLB} raised $6\!\to\!7$---which doubles
delivered predictions per cycle from 16 to 32---plus three changes that do not
touch the tagged tables at all: \texttt{LOGB} $12\!\to\!14$, \texttt{GHIST}
$100\!\to\!102$ and \texttt{GHIST1} $6\!\to\!8$. It is \emph{not} more accurate
than the seed---\mpki{} rises from $5.338$ to $5.354$---and at $38.0$\,KiB
against the seed's $35.0$\,KiB it buys its $5.74\%$ score improvement almost
entirely with width. Conversely the most accurate design found,
\texttt{gen010\_2} at $\mpki{}=5.084$, spends $74.25$\,KiB, more than twice the
seed, and scores only $\vfs{}=0.7444$.

The point survives at the level of the whole archive, and more sharply than at
generation ten. Every one of the seven elites holding a cell at generation~29
has $\texttt{LOGLB}=7$: the search converged unanimously on the widest fetch
line the template allows, in every corner of the behaviour space, while their
\mpki{} values sit within $0.4$ of one another. Width is the only axis the
score consistently pays for. Storage is not that axis either---the second-best
elite, \texttt{gen017\_2} at $\vfs{}=0.9207$, is \emph{smaller} than the seed
at $26.0$\,KiB.

A single derived parameter explains the accuracy axis. The number of hashed tag
bits is $H = \texttt{TAGW} - (\texttt{LOGLB}-2)$, and \mpki{} is a step
function of it: the 71 designs with $H\ge 6$ average $5.41$ \mpki{} and never
exceed $5.95$; the 15 with $H\le5$ average $9.44$ and never fall below $9.00$.
Across 86 designs nothing lands in the gap. Tag aliasing is a cliff, not a
slope, and the cliff edge is between $H=5$ and $H=6$.

\subsection{Finding 2: the gem5 tier does not discriminate}

Across the 29 designs promoted to Tier~2, gem5 core IPC at the shipped depth
spans $0.8867$--$0.8920$: a total spread of $0.60\%$. Tripling the promoted
population did not improve separation---it widened the range by five parts in
ten thousand. Tier~2 as configured cannot separate these designs, and this must
be stated before any correlation computed on it is believed.

It is worth showing why. Regressing Tier-1 \mpki{} against gem5 depth-9 IPC
over all 29 designs gives $r=-0.761$, which reads as respectable
cross-simulator agreement. It is not robust. Deleting \texttt{gen005\_0}
alone (Tier-1 $\mpki{}=16.15$, the only design far from the pack) collapses it
to $-0.017$; deleting \texttt{gen005\_0} and \texttt{gen008\_1} together
\emph{inverts} it to $+0.167$. Over all $\binom{29}{2}$ leave-two-out subsets
the coefficient ranges from $-0.889$ to $+0.167$---it changes sign. That two
points out of 29 still control the sign is a stronger indictment than the
same behaviour at ten points, not a weaker one. We report it because the per-generation
$\tau(\vfs{},\text{IPC})=+1.00$ printed by our own harness is likewise
degenerate, computed over the single design promoted per generation, and should
not be quoted.

the shipped depth of nine by Kendall's $\tau$ over all 21 pairs. Running the
search a further nineteen generations turned this measurement into a natural
experiment, because the archive it is computed over is not the same set of
designs it was at generation ten (Table~\ref{tab:tau}).

At generation ten the decay was not diffuse: \emph{every} discordant pair, at
every depth, had \texttt{gen006\_2} on one side, and removing that one design
restored $\tau=+1.000$ at all eight depths. \texttt{gen006\_2} was precisely
the archived elite with high \mpki{}---$9.220$ against $5.08$--$5.46$ for the
other six. Its tag width had been cut to $\texttt{TAGW}=8$, giving $H=4$ hashed
tag bits and eight times the tag aliasing of the seed, while its first-level
gshare was doubled: accuracy traded for bandwidth.

By generation~29 that design is gone. MAP-Elites evicted it in the ordinary
way---a higher-scoring design landed in its cell---and with it went every
inversion. The generation-29 archive spans $\mpki{}$ $5.23$--$5.62$, a factor
of $1.07$ rather than $1.8$, and its ranking is exactly stable at every depth
from six to thirty. The one discordant pair anywhere in the table is at depth
three, between \texttt{gen027\_2} and \texttt{gen025\_0}, whose depth-nine
scores differ by $0.003$; removing either restores $\tau=+1.000$ throughout.

The mechanism is unchanged and is now visible from both sides. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cpi}, $C$ scales with $\text{mpi}\cdot D$, so a design with
$1.7\times$ the misprediction rate loses ground $1.7\times$ faster as $D$ grows
and must eventually cross the others; at generation ten, depth 16 was where it
did. When the archive holds an accuracy outlier the depth-nine ranking does not
survive a deeper machine; when it holds none, the ranking is stable to depth
thirty. Depth robustness is a direct function of accuracy---and accuracy is
exactly the quantity Finding 1 shows the depth-nine score does not measure.

This sharpens rather than softens the concern. Nothing the search or the score
could observe distinguishes the two archives: both were produced by the same
operator climbing the same objective, and \vfs{} at depth nine ranked both
without complaint. Whether the resulting ranking transfers to a different
pipeline depth was decided by which design happened to occupy one cell of the
behaviour space at the moment the archive was read. A metric whose
transferability depends on the incidental composition of the population it is
applied to cannot be checked by applying it more carefully; it has to be
reported at more than one depth.

\subsection{The archive}

Seven of 144 nominal cells are occupied, which reads as $4.9\%$ coverage and
invites the conclusion that the search barely explored. That conclusion is
wrong. Across all 86 scored designs, only two
\epi{} bands, two $p_1$ values and two $p_2$ values were ever realized: the
\emph{reachable} sub-grid is $2\times2\times2=8$ cells, and the search occupies
seven of them. The grid is mis-specified for this design space---four of six
\epi{} bands lie below any achievable predictor energy---rather than
under-explored. Tripling the population did not widen it: the same eight cells
were reachable at generation ten and at generation twenty-nine, and the same
seven were occupied. What changed is which designs hold them---four of the
seven elites were replaced---so the coverage figure is stable while its
contents are not.

That churn is the diversity mechanism doing its job, and it is also how the
archive quietly changed character. At generation ten it held \texttt{gen006\_2},
which scored \emph{worse} than the seed ($0.7855$ vs.\ $0.8784$) and survived
only because it occupied a different cell; the design that broke the depth
ranking had entered through diversity, not through the score. By generation
twenty-nine a better design had landed in that cell and evicted it. Neither
event was directed at the depth ranking, and neither was visible in the score.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\textbf{In-sample scores.} Every \vfs{} here is measured on the 126 traces the
search optimized against. The 42 held-out traces remain unscored.

\textbf{One seed, one operator.} The offline arm resizes tables; it cannot
invent an algorithm. Findings 1 and 3 concern the metric's response to a
population, and that population is a parameter family. A structurally different
predictor could move $p_2$ or \epi{} into regions we never reached.

\textbf{Tier-2 configuration, not gem5.} Finding 2 is a statement about our
gem5 configuration---$5\times10^{6}$ instructions, one promoted design per
generation, SE-mode workloads---not about gem5's capability. Tripling the
promoted population from 10 to 29 did not restore discrimination, but a longer
window or a wider promotion budget could.

\textbf{Small elite set, and an uncontrolled comparison.} Table~\ref{tab:tau}
rests on seven elites and 21 pairs at each of two points in the search. We did
not remove \texttt{gen006\_2}; MAP-Elites did, incidentally, when a
higher-scoring design landed in its cell. The two archives therefore differ in
more than the outlier, and the contrast between them is observational. What the
two columns jointly support is narrower than a controlled result: an archive
containing an accuracy outlier produced depth-dependent rankings, one without
did not, and in both cases the discordant pairs are exactly the pairs with the
largest \mpki{} gap.

\section{Conclusions}

\vfs{} is a considerable improvement on ranking predictors by misprediction
rate, but as an optimization target it behaves as a throughput metric with
minor corrections. An automated search given \vfs{} alone finds width, not
accuracy: the best design we found is the seed with a wider fetch line and
slightly worse \mpki{}. Because CBP-NG assumes a fixed nine-stage penalty, and
because misprediction cost is the only depth-dependent term, the score is
structurally blind to the one property---accuracy---that decides whether its
ranking survives a deeper machine. Whether that blindness bites is a property
of the population, not of the score: the archive at generation ten contained
one high-\mpki{} design and every rank inversion we observed involved it, while
the archive at generation twenty-nine had evicted it and ranks identically at
every depth from six to thirty. A practitioner reading \vfs{} at depth nine
cannot tell which of those two situations they are in. We suggest that cost-aware predictor metrics
be reported at more than one depth, which the re-scoring identity of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cpi} makes nearly free, and that automated searches over such
metrics report which term is actually carrying the optimizer.
